# Cross Product

Sometimes we don't have a pretty axis, like a lugnut, to reference when trying to the the moment a force generates. In cases like these we can use the cross product. What is the cross product? Mathmatically its $\vec{\mathbf{C}}=\vec{\mathbf{A}}\times{}\vec{\mathbf{B}}$ this is spoken as "C equals A cross B".

## Why do we care?
With the dot product we can determine how much of one vector acts in another vectors direction. However with a cross product we can determine how much of one vector causes rotation relative to another. This is how to truely calculate how much rotation a force can cause. 

## The Relationship
The moment of a force about a point $O$ is:
$$
\bf{M_O}=\vec{\mathbf{r}}\times{}\vec{\mathbf{F}}
$$
where:

- $M_O$ = Moment about points $O$.
- $\vec{\mathbf{r}}$ = Position vector from $O$ to any point along the force's line of action.
- $\vec{\mathbf{F}}$ = Applied Force.

Now this can easily be captured as:
$$
\mathbf{M}=rF\sin{\theta}
$$
Where $r$ and $F$ are the magntiudes of their respsective vectors.

You dont really need to calculate this directly because using matrix math or cartesian cross products will handle the geometry for you.

## Direction
Well thats really cool, we calculated a number, where is it going?

The answer is actually quite simple, OUT! Its always possible to generate a plane between two vectors, and if that plane was a sheet of paper then our cross product will point out of the page assuming were using right hand rule.

### Right Hand Rule
The Curl of your fingers will point from $\vec{\mathbf{r}}$ towards $\vec{\mathbf{F}}$, and your thumb will point in the direction of $\mathbf{M}$.

## Cartesian Cross Product
If:
$$
\mathbf{r}=r_x\mathbf{i}+r_y\mathbf{j}+r_z\mathbf{k}\ =[r_x,r_y,r_z]
$$
and
$$
\mathbf{F}=F_x\mathbf{i}+F_y\mathbf{j}+F_z\mathbf{k}\ =[F_x,F_y,F_z]
$$
then
$$
M=
\begin{bmatrix}
\mathbf{i}, & \mathbf{j}, & \mathbf{k} \\\\
r_x, & r_y, & r_z \\\\
F_x, & F_y, & F_z
\end{bmatrix}
$$
Expanded:
$$
\mathbf{M} = (r_yF_z-r_zF_y)\mathbf{i}-(r_xF_z-r_zF_x)\mathbf{j}+(r_xF_y-r_yF_x)\mathbf{k}
$$
Yes the negative sign on the $\mathbf{j}$ term is suppoed to be there.

## Important Details
The cross product is **not communicative**:
$$
\boxed{\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{B}\ne\mathbf{B}\times\mathbf{A}}
$$
However,
$$
\boxed{\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{B}=-(\mathbf{B}\times\mathbf{A})}
$$
Order **matters**
